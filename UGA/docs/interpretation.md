03\_satisfaction\_effectiveness

---

**ANOVA – Current Year of Study**
The homogeneity of variances assumption was met across all five items (Levene's p > .05 in each case). The overall ANOVA was significant only for how often results meet needs and expectations (F = 7.689, df = 2, p = .001). Post-hoc Tukey tests revealed that III-Year students (M = 1.62) reported significantly lower agreement than both I Year (M = 2.07, p = .015) and II Year (M = 2.29, p = .009) students, with I and II Year not differing from each other. The homogeneous subsets confirm III Year forming a distinct lower subset. For the remaining four items importance, effectiveness, positive impact on performance, and satisfaction no significant differences emerged across year groups (all p > .05), and each falls within a single homogeneous subset, indicating these perceptions are established early and remain stable as students progress through their academic years.
**ANOVA – Academic Stream**
The homogeneity of variances assumption was violated for importance (Levene's p < .001) and for meeting needs and expectations (Levene's p = .028), so ANOVA results for these two items should be read alongside their Kruskal-Wallis counterparts. The overall ANOVA was significant for positive impact on academic performance (F = 3.219, df = 3, p = .026), with BA students (M = 2.44) scoring significantly higher than BSc students (M = 1.00, p = .022). It was also significant for meeting needs and expectations (F = 3.409, df = 3, p = .021), with BSc lowest and BBA highest, though no individual Tukey pair reached significance at the .05 level. For the remaining three items importance, effectiveness, and satisfaction no significant differences were found across streams (all p > .05), indicating these perceptions are broadly consistent across disciplinary backgrounds.
**Correlation Analysis**
Pearson and Spearman correlations among the five perception items were all positive and significant at the 0.01 level, with p-values confirming that none of these relationships are due to chance. Effectiveness showed the strongest association with satisfaction (r = .430, ρ = .476, both p < .001), and meeting needs/expectations correlated similarly strongly with satisfaction (r = .448, ρ = .453, both p < .001) together indicating that practical effectiveness and outcome-fit are the dimensions most closely tied to overall satisfaction. Importance showed comparatively weaker, though still significant, associations with the other variables (r = .246–.381). The close agreement between Pearson and Spearman values across all pairs confirms these relationships are linear and robust.
**Regression Analysis**
A multiple regression predicting satisfaction from importance, effectiveness, positive impact on performance, and meeting needs/expectations produced a significant model (F = 9.097, df = 4, p < .001), explaining 27.7% of the variance in satisfaction (R² = .277, Adjusted R² = .247). Effectiveness (Beta = .284, p = .005) and meeting needs/expectations (Beta = .311, p = .004) emerged as significant positive predictors, while importance (Beta = .064, p = .517) and positive impact on performance (Beta = -.008, p = .934) did not contribute independently once the other variables were accounted for. All VIF values remained below 1.43, indicating no multicollinearity concerns. This suggests satisfaction is driven primarily by practical effectiveness and outcome-fit, rather than by how important students consider Google apps in the abstract.
**Kruskal-Wallis Tests**
The nonparametric results largely confirm the ANOVA findings. By year of study, meeting needs/expectations remained significant (χ² = 11.955, df = 2, p = .003), consistent with the ANOVA, while the other four items stayed non-significant. By stream, both positive impact on performance (χ² = 8.983, df = 3, p = .030) and meeting needs/expectations (χ² = 9.943, df = 3, p = .019) were confirmed significant, aligning with and reinforcing the ANOVA pattern for these two items despite the variance-equality violations noted earlier. This convergence between parametric and non-parametric tests supports the reliability of the significant findings.

Awareness and usage of additional Google tools vary considerably across the eight tools examined. **Tool Awareness by Academic Stream**
Stream proved a much stronger differentiator than year of study. Significant associations emerged for Google News (χ² = 8.930, df = 3, p = .030), Google Finance (χ² = 8.366, p = .039), Google Fonts (χ² = 10.375, p = .016), Google Scholar (χ² = 9.760, p = .021), and G Board (χ² = 10.684, p = .014), while Socratic (χ² = 3.471, p = .325), Google Translate (χ² = 1.455, p = .693), and Google Forms (χ² = 3.942, p = .685) showed no significant relationship with stream. The overall ANOVA was significant for Google News (F = 3.138, df = 3, p = .029), Google Finance (F = 2.922, p = .038), Google Fonts (F = 3.704, p = .014), Google Scholar (F = 3.461, p = .019), and G Board (F = 3.828, p = .012), while Socratic (F = 1.151, p = .333), Google Translate (F = 0.473, p = .702), and Google Forms (F = 0.711, p = .548) were non-significant. This indicates a specific pairwise difference exists in each case, but not a fully separated grouping of streams a more conservative reading that is consistent with the Levene's violations noted above, and these three findings should be presented with that caveat.

Google Forms was the only tool to show a significant difference by year (F = 3.579, df = 2, p = .032), with Tukey post-hoc showing III Year students significantly more aware of Forms than I Year students (p = .033) a sensible pattern, since Forms usage likely accumulates with academic experience through assignments, feedback surveys, and event registrations. All other tools showed no significant difference by year, reinforcing that year of study is not a meaningful driver of tool awareness beyond this one case.

Awareness of the more specialized tools clusters together: Google Finance correlates positively with Fonts, Scholar, Socratic, and G Board (r = .30–.49, all p < .01), indicating that students familiar with one advanced Google tool tend to be familiar with others as part of a broader pattern of digital exploration. News and Forms are also positively related (r = .327, ρ = .357, both p ≤ .001). One notable exception is a significant negative correlation between Socratic and Google Translate (r = -.252, ρ = -.252, p = .011), suggesting these may appeal to different student segments rather than reflecting the general awareness cluster. The close alignment between Pearson and Spearman values throughout confirms these relationships are robust rather than an artifact of the data's distribution.
**Reliability Analysis – Tool Awareness Index**
The eight-item awareness scale showed a Cronbach's Alpha of .700, meeting the conventional 0.70 threshold for acceptable internal consistency, with a scale mean of 13.53 and standard deviation of 3.73. This indicates the items cohere reasonably well as a single measure of overall Google ecosystem awareness, though the alpha sits at the borderline of acceptability rather than comfortably above it plausible given that the eight tools serve quite different functions (translation, finance, research, keyboard input) rather than tapping a single narrow construct.
